# SCOTUS Chat Audience

This document defines who SCOTUS Chat is for, what is known about those readers,
and what remains hypothetical. It should help future product, homepage, design,
and research decisions stay honest about the audience instead of inventing a
market before one has been validated.

## Audience Summary

SCOTUS Chat is first an audience-of-one product.

The product began because the creator wanted to read Supreme Court oral argument
transcripts and found the traditional format cognitively difficult to follow.
The initial audience is therefore concrete: a reader who cares enough about an
argument to read the transcript, but needs a clearer way to track speaker
identity, role, side, and conversational flow.

The broader public audience is a hypothesis: other people may benefit from the
same reading format, especially if they experience similar friction with dense
legal transcripts.

## Primary Audience: The Creator

The creator is the first validated user.

Known needs:

- Wants to read Supreme Court oral argument transcripts directly.
- Finds traditional transcript formatting difficult to track.
- Benefits from seeing speaker identity attached to each utterance.
- Benefits from visual distinction between speakers.
- Benefits from bench and advocates being separated by side.
- Benefits from on-demand speaker context instead of memorizing details.
- Wants the material presented neutrally, without political or legal commentary.

This audience matters because it grounds the product in a real use case. SCOTUS
Chat does not need to start by pretending to serve everyone. It should first
serve this reader well, then learn which adjacent audiences share similar needs.

## Core Reader Need

The common need is not simply interest in the Supreme Court. The common need is
a clearer way to follow a long, dense, speaker-heavy public transcript.

A likely SCOTUS Chat reader may be asking:

- Who is speaking now?
- Is this person a Justice or an advocate?
- Which side does this advocate represent?
- What role does this person have in this argument?
- How did this exchange move from one speaker to the next?
- Where am I in the argument?
- How can I return to this moment later?

The product should prioritize readers who need help with orientation and flow,
not readers who primarily want analysis, commentary, or conclusions.

## Broader Audience Hypotheses

These groups may benefit from SCOTUS Chat, but their needs should be treated as
hypotheses until tested.

### Neurodivergent Readers

SCOTUS Chat may help readers with ADHD, AuDHD, working memory limitations, or
other cognitive load sensitivities because the interface externalizes details
that traditional transcripts ask readers to hold internally.

Potential needs:

- Reduced working memory burden.
- Stronger visual anchors for speaker identity.
- Easier re-orientation after attention shifts.
- Less effort spent decoding transcript structure.
- Clear separation between source material and surrounding context.

Current confidence: high as an internal hypothesis, unvalidated externally.

### Legal Students And Learners

Students or self-directed learners may use SCOTUS Chat to follow oral argument
structure before they have fluency with court conventions.

Potential needs:

- Clear speaker roles.
- Visible sides and participant context.
- Easier movement through long transcripts.
- Ability to connect a speaker's utterance to their procedural role.

Current confidence: plausible, but untested.

### Researchers And Legal Professionals

Researchers, lawyers, academics, or legal professionals may use SCOTUS Chat as a
reading or reference layer over source transcripts, especially when they need to
locate who said what quickly.

Potential needs:

- Accurate source linkage.
- Stable URLs for arguments or utterances.
- Search by case, docket, speaker, term, or phrase.
- Confidence that the product is not editorializing the transcript.

Current confidence: plausible for reference use, but the product should not
claim to be a full legal research platform.

### Journalists And Civic Educators

Journalists, educators, and civic communicators may use SCOTUS Chat to inspect
or reference public arguments without relying on excerpted coverage.

Potential needs:

- Fast orientation to argument participants.
- Shareable source locations.
- Plain descriptions of what the page contains.
- Neutral presentation that does not imply a political frame.

Current confidence: plausible, especially for citation and orientation, but
untested.

### Curious Members Of The Public

Some readers may arrive from search, social media, or a direct link because they
want to understand what was said in a particular argument.

Potential needs:

- Immediate explanation of what the site is.
- Low-friction entry into the transcript.
- Clear case, docket, date, and speaker context.
- No assumption of legal background.

Current confidence: plausible, but public readers may need more orientation
than the creator does.

## Who The Product Is Not Primarily For

SCOTUS Chat should not optimize for readers who mainly want:

- Case summaries.
- Political commentary.
- Predictions about outcomes.
- Ideological analysis of Justices.
- Rankings of important moments.
- Argument scores or persuasion metrics.
- A replacement for comprehensive legal research tools.

These people may still use the site, but their goals should not drive the core
product direction.

## Accessibility Hypothesis

The central accessibility hypothesis is:

> A chat-style transcript interface can reduce cognitive load by making speaker
> identity, role, side, and conversational flow visible at the point of reading.

This hypothesis comes from the creator's lived use case and prior prototypes. It
is strong enough to guide design decisions, but not yet strong enough to support
broad public claims about neurodivergent accessibility outcomes.

A future research effort should test this hypothesis with external readers,
especially readers with ADHD, AuDHD, working memory challenges, or similar
cognitive load sensitivities.

## Audience Implications For The Homepage

The homepage should not assume a single professional audience. It should orient
several possible readers without becoming generic.

It should answer quickly:

- What is this?
- What material does it contain?
- How is it different from a normal transcript?
- How do I find an argument?
- Can I trust that it is not commentary?

It should avoid audience claims like:

- "Built for legal researchers."
- "The public's guide to the Supreme Court."
- "Accessibility for neurodivergent readers."
- "The easiest way for everyone to understand oral arguments."

For now, better public framing is:

- "A clearer way to read Supreme Court oral argument transcripts."
- "Follow who said what, with speakers, roles, and sides visible as you read."
- "Public transcripts presented as structured conversations, without added
  commentary."

## What We Do Not Know Yet

Open audience questions:

- Do other neurodivergent readers experience the same benefits as the creator?
- Which layout choices reduce working memory burden most effectively?
- Do legal students need more explanatory context than the current interface
  provides?
- Do researchers value utterance-level sharing or citation links?
- Do public readers understand the difference between a case, an argument, a
  docket, and a term?
- How much homepage explanation is enough before it becomes clutter?

These questions should inform future research, testing, and homepage design.

## Audience Principle

SCOTUS Chat should serve the known reader honestly before claiming to serve an
imagined market broadly.

The strongest audience posture is:

> Built from one real need, made public in case the same format helps others.
