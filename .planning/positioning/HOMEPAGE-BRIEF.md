# SCOTUS Chat Homepage Brief

This brief defines what the SCOTUS Chat homepage should accomplish before any
visual design, Figma exploration, or implementation work begins. It translates
the foundation, principles, voice, and audience documents into homepage
requirements.

The homepage should be treated as an orientation surface, not a marketing
landing page.

## Homepage Job

The homepage should quickly help a visitor understand:

1. What SCOTUS Chat is.
2. What material it contains.
3. How the format helps.
4. How to find an argument.
5. Why the presentation can be trusted as non-editorial.

Its job is not to persuade visitors that Supreme Court arguments matter, explain
the Court, summarize cases, or promote AI. Its job is to give readers a clear
path into public oral argument transcripts presented in a more followable form.

## Core Homepage Promise

The homepage should communicate this promise:

> SCOTUS Chat presents Supreme Court oral arguments as structured
> conversations, making it easier to follow who said what without adding
> commentary or political framing.

This promise should guide the hero, navigation, browse areas, and supporting
copy.

## First-Screen Requirements

The first screen should answer the basic orientation question before asking the
visitor to interpret the interface.

It should include:

- The product name.
- A plain explanation of the format.
- A direct path to search or browse arguments.
- Enough visual or textual signal to show that this is about oral argument
  transcripts, not commentary.
- A calm, source-oriented tone.

It should avoid:

- A dramatic featured case as the main editorial hook.
- A quote pulled from an argument for emotional or political impact.
- AI-first messaging.
- Broad accessibility claims that have not been externally validated.
- Any implication that the site identifies key moments or winning arguments.

## Primary User Paths

The homepage should support three primary paths.

### 1. Find A Known Argument

For visitors who arrive with a case, docket, speaker, or term in mind.

Homepage support:

- Prominent search.
- Clear placeholder copy such as "Search cases, dockets, speakers, or terms."
- Fast route to matching arguments.

### 2. Browse The Archive

For visitors who do not have a specific argument in mind but want to see what is
available.

Homepage support:

- Recent or available arguments.
- Browse by term.
- Clear counts or availability indicators if useful.
- No editorial ordering unless the basis is explicit, such as date or term.

### 3. Understand The Format

For visitors who need to know why this is different from a normal transcript.

Homepage support:

- Brief explanation of speaker identity, roles, sides, and conversation flow.
- Small, concrete examples of interface benefits without quoting argument text
  for drama.
- Link to a fuller About or methodology page if one exists later.

## Content Priority

Homepage content should be prioritized in this order:

1. Clear statement of what the site is.
2. Search or browse entry point.
3. Recent or available arguments.
4. Browse by term or archive navigation.
5. Short explanation of the format.
6. Trust and source notes.
7. About/project context.

The homepage should not bury the argument list behind a long mission statement.
The product becomes understandable fastest when visitors can see and enter the
archive.

## Recommended Content Blocks

### Product Header

Purpose:

- Identify the product.
- State the core promise.
- Provide immediate search or browse access.

Likely copy direction:

> Supreme Court oral arguments, presented as structured conversations.

Supporting copy should mention speaker identity, roles, and flow rather than
analysis or AI.

### Search And Browse Entry

Purpose:

- Help the visitor find a known argument.
- Signal that the site is functional and archive-oriented.

Search should support cases, dockets, speakers, and terms if the product can
actually deliver those results.

### Recent Or Available Arguments

Purpose:

- Make the archive tangible.
- Give visitors a low-friction entry point.

Ordering should be chronological, term-based, or otherwise transparent. Avoid
labels such as "featured," "important," "notable," or "must-read."

### Browse By Term

Purpose:

- Support visitors who think in Court terms or want a broad archive view.
- Provide structure without editorial hierarchy.

Term cards or rows should be compact, factual, and scannable.

### How The Format Helps

Purpose:

- Explain why the chat-style presentation exists.
- Connect the interface to reduced cognitive load without making unvalidated
  accessibility claims.

Possible points:

- Speaker identity stays visible.
- Bench and advocates are visually separated.
- Speaker context is available on demand.
- Long arguments are easier to re-enter and navigate.

### Trust / Source Note

Purpose:

- Reinforce that SCOTUS Chat is source-oriented and non-editorial.

Possible content:

- Uses public Supreme Court oral argument transcripts.
- Reformats transcripts for readability.
- Does not add summaries, analysis, rankings, or political commentary.
- Links to source transcripts where available.

### About This Project

Purpose:

- Provide a modest explanation of the project's origin and intent.
- Keep deeper personal/accessibility context available without making it the
  main homepage burden.

This section should be short on the homepage, with room for a fuller About page
later.

## Design Implications

The homepage should feel like a trustworthy reading tool and archive.

Design should favor:

- Calm density.
- Fast scanning.
- Clear hierarchy.
- Stable navigation.
- Source-oriented presentation.
- Equal treatment of cases and speakers.
- Functional calls to action.

Design should avoid:

- Oversized marketing hero sections that delay access to the archive.
- Editorial pull quotes.
- Dramatic case imagery.
- Decorative AI or technology motifs.
- Engagement-oriented sharing prompts.
- Any visual treatment that makes one case or side feel more important.

## Copy Guidelines

Homepage copy should follow `VOICE.md`.

Use language like:

- "Read oral arguments as structured conversations."
- "Follow who said what."
- "Browse arguments by term."
- "View the source transcript."
- "Public transcripts, reformatted for clarity."

Avoid language like:

- "AI-powered analysis."
- "Discover the key moments."
- "Understand what the Justices really think."
- "The definitive Supreme Court archive."
- "Making the Court understandable for everyone."

## Success Criteria

A successful homepage should let a first-time visitor answer:

- This is a site for reading Supreme Court oral argument transcripts.
- The transcripts are presented as structured conversations.
- Speaker identity, roles, and sides are easier to track.
- The site is not offering commentary or case analysis.
- I can search or browse to find an argument.

It should let a returning visitor answer:

- Where do I search?
- Where are recent or available arguments?
- How do I browse by term?
- How do I return to an argument quickly?

## Open Questions

These questions should be resolved before final homepage design:

- Should the homepage lead with search, recent arguments, or both?
- How much archive inventory will exist at launch?
- Which search dimensions are available on day one?
- Will source transcript links be available for every public argument?
- Should the homepage include the creator-origin story, or reserve that for an
  About page?
- How much explanation is needed for "term," "argument," "case," and "docket"?
- Should utterance-level sharing be hinted at only inside argument pages, not on
  the homepage?

## Working Direction

The strongest current direction is:

> Archive-first, search-forward, source-oriented, and calm.

The homepage should help people enter the material quickly while making the
format and non-editorial boundary clear.
