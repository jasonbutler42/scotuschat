# SCOTUS Chat Messaging

This document collects reusable messaging for SCOTUS Chat. It is an internal
copy source for homepage exploration, public-page copy, About content, metadata,
interface labels, and future design work.

All copy should be evaluated against `FOUNDATION.md`, `PRINCIPLES.md`,
`VOICE.md`, `AUDIENCE.md`, and `HOMEPAGE-BRIEF.md` before publication.

## Messaging Strategy

The core message is simple:

> SCOTUS Chat presents Supreme Court oral arguments as structured conversations,
> making it easier to follow who said what without adding commentary or
> political framing.

The public-facing story should emphasize:

- Public oral argument transcripts.
- Speaker identity, roles, sides, and flow.
- A clearer reading format.
- Source-oriented, non-editorial presentation.
- Search and browsing as the way into the archive.

The public-facing story should not emphasize:

- AI as the main product value.
- Legal analysis.
- Political interpretation.
- Case summaries.
- Accessibility outcomes that have not been externally validated.

These may be useful in some contexts, but should be checked carefully:

- The Court, easier to follow.
- Legal transcripts without the maze.

Risks:

- "The Court" can sound too broad.
- "Legal transcripts" can overgeneralize beyond Supreme Court oral arguments.
- "Calmer" may be accurate but feels more subjective.
- "Transcript friction" is useful internally but may feel abstract publicly.

## What This Is / Is Not

### Compact Version

SCOTUS Chat is a clearer interface for public Supreme Court oral argument
transcripts. It helps readers follow who said what. It is not legal analysis,
political commentary, or a case summary service.

### Structured Version

SCOTUS Chat is:

- A reading interface for public Supreme Court oral argument transcripts.
- A way to follow speaker identity, roles, sides, and argument flow.
- A source-oriented archive organized around arguments, speakers, and terms.
- A tool for reading the transcript, not replacing it.

SCOTUS Chat is not:

- A legal analysis product.
- A political commentary site.
- A case summary service.
- A ranking of important moments.
- A prediction or persuasion tool.
- A replacement for legal research platforms.

## About Copy

### Short About

SCOTUS Chat exists because oral argument transcripts are public, important, and
harder to read than they need to be. The project presents Supreme Court oral
arguments as structured conversations so readers can follow speaker identity,
roles, sides, and flow without added commentary or political framing.

### Origin-Aware About

SCOTUS Chat began with a concrete reading problem: traditional oral argument
transcripts can make it difficult to track who is speaking and how the exchange
is moving. The project reformats public Supreme Court transcripts as structured
conversations, keeping speaker identity and role context visible while leaving
the legal meaning to the reader.

### Longer About

SCOTUS Chat is an access-oriented reading interface for Supreme Court oral
argument transcripts. It presents public transcripts as structured
conversations, making it easier to follow who is speaking, what role they have,
which side they are associated with, and how the exchange moves between the
bench and advocates.

The project is built around a narrow promise: clarify the structure of the
source material without interpreting its meaning. SCOTUS Chat does not summarize
arguments, identify key moments, rank speakers, infer ideology, or add political
commentary. It helps readers stay oriented while reading the public transcript.

## Copy To Avoid

Avoid copy that suggests SCOTUS Chat is interpreting, ranking, or editorializing
source material:

- Discover the most important Supreme Court moments.
- Understand what the Justices really think.
- See who won the argument.
- Track ideological signals.
- Explore the Court's biggest debates.
- The definitive guide to Supreme Court arguments.
- Making Supreme Court law simple.
