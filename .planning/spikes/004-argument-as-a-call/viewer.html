What the rules forced, and what they forbade
  • No speaking-time meter — deliberately. Watch either arrangement and you will form a clear impression of who dominated this stretch. That impression is the point: the project may never assert it, but a faithful rendering the reader interprets is fair game. What is forbidden is amplifying it — no tile scaled by cumulative talk time, no reordering by participation, no "most active" badge. Render at fidelity; if a design makes the disparity easier to read than it was in the room, it has crossed the line.
  • Emphasis is transient, never structural. Every tile is identical in size, weight and treatment when nobody is speaking. The active-speaker highlight is a fact about the present moment and it moves on. Nobody is permanently larger, centred or first.
  • Initials for everyone, not photographs. Justices have good portrait coverage and advocates do not. At tile size, a bench of faces opposite a row of blanks would read as a status difference produced by nothing but data coverage.
  • Tile order is first appearance, not seniority. The real bench is seated by seniority, and reproducing that would be faithful — but it is not in this data, and inventing a seating chart would be fabrication. The arc here tests the geometry, not the seats.
  • Colour is per speaker, from the shipped ramp. Every hue is solved to CIE L* 78, so no justice or advocate reads louder than any other. These are the same seven assignments the live transcript uses.
  • The question to actually answer. The grid is more familiar; the courtroom keeps the one thing that has been constant across this whole project — the bench on one side, advocates on the other. A uniform grid erases that. Which matters more is the decision, not the chrome.
